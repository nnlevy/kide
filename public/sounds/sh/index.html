Speech sounds

The “sh” sound

Most children can say “sh” reliably by about 5 years old. If yours can't yet and they're younger than that, they are on time.

The short answer

“sh” is one of the later sounds, and a four-year-old saying “soo” for “shoe” is on time. The rounded lips are the half you can see, which makes them the half you can help with.

How “sh” is made

The tongue pulls back slightly from where it sits for "s", and — the part you can see — the lips push forward and round.

You can show part of this one. Some of what matters happens on the front of the face and some of it happens behind the teeth, so a mirror helps a little — but do not expect watching alone to be enough.

The half of "sh" you can actually see

"sh" is made of two things happening together, and children very often get one and not the other. The tongue position is hidden and hard; the lip rounding is right there on the front of the face and easy to copy. So the common near-miss is a sound sitting somewhere between "s" and "sh" — the tongue still forward, the lips already correct. That is a child halfway there rather than a child who is missing the sound, and the visible half is the half you can help with.

Try this. The "quiet" gesture — finger to lips, a long "shhhh". Children already know this one socially, which means they will produce it without it feeling like practice at all.

Where the “sh” figure of 5 years old comes from

McLeod & Crowe (2018) reviewed consonant acquisition across 64 studies and reported the age by which 90% of typically developing children produce each sound correctly — in the beginning, middle and end of words. For “sh” that age is 5;00 (5 years old).

The 90% figure is deliberately conservative. Older charts used 50% or 75%, which produce much earlier ages and flag ordinary late acquisition as a problem. We use the conservative number on purpose: an earlier one would manufacture worry rather than answer it.

If your child says “soo” instead of “shoe”

Swapping “s” in for “sh” is not a random mistake. It has a name — palatal fronting — and it is one of the most common and best documented patterns in young children's speech. Nearly every child does some version of it.

It usually sorts itself out by around 4 and a half. Before then it is a normal stage, not an error to be corrected. If it is still there well after that, it is worth mentioning — not because something is wrong, but because that is when someone should take a proper look.

Don't correct it in the moment. Saying “no, say shoe” teaches a child that talking is a test they can fail, and a child who thinks that talks less. Say the word yourself, warmly, and carry on — they get the model without the verdict.

How to practise without it becoming a test

Say the word yourself first, clearly and slowly. Let your child try. If what comes back isn't right, don't correct it — say the word again warmly, and carry on. A child who feels tested stops trying, and a child who stops trying gets no practice at all.

Common questions

My child's "sh" sounds like something between "s" and "sh".

That is the usual near-miss, and it is a good sign. "sh" needs the lips rounded and the tongue pulled back; the lips are visible and get learned first. Exaggerate the round lips when you say the word and the visible half takes care of itself.

My child says "soo" instead of "shoe". Is that normal?

Yes — very. Substituting "s" for "sh" is a documented pattern called palatal fronting, and it typically resolves on its own by around 4 and a half. Don't correct it in the moment; just say the word yourself and move on.

When should my child be able to say "sh"?

Around 5 years old. That figure is the 90% mastery age from McLeod & Crowe (2018), meaning 90% of typically developing children produce "sh" correctly in every position in a word by then. The 10% who take longer are still typical.

My child is younger than that and can't say "sh". Should I worry?

No. A child below the expected age who isn't producing a sound is doing exactly what the research predicts. It is worth mentioning to your doctor if the sound is still absent well after 5 years old, or if you or other people often can't understand what your child is saying — that second one matters more than any single sound.

How do I practise the "sh" sound at home?

Say the word yourself, clearly and unhurried, and let your child have a go. Don't correct a wrong attempt — say the word again warmly and move on. Practice works because it is repeated and pleasant, not because it is accurate. Kide is built on exactly that rule: nothing is scored, and there is no way to fail.

Which words are best for practising "sh"?

Short, concrete words for things a child can picture: shine, sheep, fish, push. Every word on this page was checked to be sure the "sh" sound really does occur where we say it does.
