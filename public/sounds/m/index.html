Speech sounds

The “m” sound

Children can usually say “m” reliably by about 3 years old. Crowe & McLeod (2020) place the 90% mean for this sound in the 2;0–2;11 band among children acquiring English in the United States (https://doi.org/10.1044/2020_AJSLP-19-00168). If yours can't yet and they're younger than that, they are on time.

The short answer

“m” is one of the earliest sounds of all, usually there by around 3 years old. If it vanishes for a week, check whether your child has a cold before you consider anything else — this is the first sound a blocked nose takes away. Crowe & McLeod (2020) (https://doi.org/10.1044/2020_AJSLP-19-00168).

How “m” is made

Lips closed, voice on, and the air goes out through the nose instead of the mouth.

This one is easy to show. Sit somewhere you can both see a mirror, or just get face to face, and let your child watch you make it — everything that matters happens on the front of your face.

A sound you can make with your mouth shut

Ask your child to hum with their lips together and they are already making a perfect "m" — it needs no tongue placement, no air stream through the mouth, and no timing. What it does need is a clear nose. That is why "m" is the first sound to vanish during a cold: "bummy" for "mummy" during a blocked week is plumbing, not speech development, and it comes back with the sniffle.

Try this. Hum a tune together with lips closed, then open your lips mid-hum. The hum turns into a vowel and back. Children find the switch funny and it is the sound itself.

Where this age comes from

McLeod & Crowe (2018) reviewed 60 articles describing 64 studies of 26,007 children in 27 languages (https://doi.org/10.1044/2018_AJSLP-17-0100). For English, using a 90%–100% criterion across eight studies, they report mean ages in year-long bands. When a study's ages differed by word position, they recorded the youngest age. They did not use an all-positions rule for every study.

For “m”, Crowe & McLeod (2020) place the 90% mean in the 2;0–2;11 band (https://doi.org/10.1044/2020_AJSLP-19-00168). “By about 3 years old” is the birthday just after that band, which is the conservative way to say it to a parent. It is not a claim that every study found that exact month.

A 50% or 75% criterion lands earlier. Crowe & McLeod (2020) tabulate those criteria next to the 90% figures (https://doi.org/10.1044/2020_AJSLP-19-00168). We talk about the later criterion so an early chart does not turn ordinary acquisition into a worry.

How to practice without it becoming a test

Say the word yourself first, clearly and slowly. Let your child try. If what comes back isn't right, don't correct it — say the word again warmly, and carry on. A child who feels tested stops trying, and a child who stops trying gets no practice at all.

Common questions

My child says "bummy" instead of "mummy" when they have a cold. Should I worry?

No. "m" needs air to leave through the nose, so a blocked nose has nowhere to send it and the sound turns into "b". It is mechanical and it comes back when the cold does.

When should my child be able to say "m"?

Around 3 years old. Crowe & McLeod (2020) (https://doi.org/10.1044/2020_AJSLP-19-00168) place the 90% mean for this sound in the 2;0–2;11 band for children acquiring English in the United States. Children who take longer are still typical.

My child is younger than that and can't say "m". Should I worry?

No. A child younger than the age above who isn't producing the sound is doing what the studies describe. It is worth mentioning to your doctor if the sound is still absent well after 3 years old, or if you or other people often can't understand what your child is saying — that second one matters more than any single sound. Crowe & McLeod (2020) (https://doi.org/10.1044/2020_AJSLP-19-00168).

How do I practice the "m" sound at home?

Say the word yourself, clearly and unhurried, and let your child have a go. Don't correct a wrong attempt — say the word again warmly and move on. Practice works because it is repeated and pleasant, not because it is accurate. Kide is built on exactly that rule: nothing is scored, and there is no way to fail.

Which words to practice for "m"?

Short, concrete words for things a child can picture: mat, moth, moon, match, mouse. Every word on this page was checked to be sure the "m" sound really does occur where we say it does.
