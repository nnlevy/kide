Speech sounds

The “p” sound

Most children can say “p” reliably by about 3 years old. If yours can't yet and they're younger than that, they are on time.

The short answer

“p” is one of the first sounds children get, usually by around 3 years old. If what you are hearing is “big” for “pig”, the lips are already doing the right thing — only the voice is in the wrong place.

How “p” is made

Both lips press shut, pressure builds behind them, and they pop apart with a little burst of air.

This one is easy to show. Sit somewhere you can both see a mirror, or just get face to face, and let your child watch you make it — everything that matters happens on the front of your face.

“p” and “b” are the same movement. For both, the lips do exactly the same thing — the voice is switched off for "p" and on for "b". Put a hand on your throat and say them one after the other: one buzzes and one doesn't. If your child has one of the pair they already own the mouth position for the other, which is a much smaller gap than it sounds.

A puff of air you can actually see

Hold a strip of tissue paper in front of your mouth and say "pig". It flutters. Say "big" and it barely moves. That puff is the entire difference between the two sounds — the lips, the tongue and the jaw are doing something identical. So a child who says "big" for "pig" is not missing a sound at all. They have the whole mouth movement already and are simply leaving the voice running through it, which is why this one tends to sort itself out early and quietly.

Try this. Tear a strip of tissue, hold it at your lips, and take turns making it dance. It turns an invisible difference into a game with a visible answer.

Where the “p” figure of 3 years old comes from

McLeod & Crowe (2018) reviewed consonant acquisition across 64 studies and reported the age by which 90% of typically developing children produce each sound correctly — in the beginning, middle and end of words. For “p” that age is 3;00 (3 years old).

The 90% figure is deliberately conservative. Older charts used 50% or 75%, which produce much earlier ages and flag ordinary late acquisition as a problem. We use the conservative number on purpose: an earlier one would manufacture worry rather than answer it.

If your child says “big” instead of “pig”

Swapping “b” in for “p” is not a random mistake. It has a name — voicing — and it is one of the most common and best documented patterns in young children's speech. Nearly every child does some version of it.

It usually sorts itself out by around 3 years old. Before then it is a normal stage, not an error to be corrected. If it is still there well after that, it is worth mentioning — not because something is wrong, but because that is when someone should take a proper look.

Don't correct it in the moment. Saying “no, say pig” teaches a child that talking is a test they can fail, and a child who thinks that talks less. Say the word yourself, warmly, and carry on — they get the model without the verdict.

How to practise without it becoming a test

Say the word yourself first, clearly and slowly. Let your child try. If what comes back isn't right, don't correct it — say the word again warmly, and carry on. A child who feels tested stops trying, and a child who stops trying gets no practice at all.

Common questions

My child says "big" instead of "pig". Is that a problem with their lips?

No — the lips are doing the right thing. "p" and "b" are made identically; the only difference is whether the voice box is running. Your child has the movement and is leaving the voice on. That is called voicing, and it is one of the earliest patterns to settle on its own.

My child says "big" instead of "pig". Is that normal?

Yes — very. Substituting "b" for "p" is a documented pattern called voicing, and it typically resolves on its own by around 3 years old. Don't correct it in the moment; just say the word yourself and move on.

When should my child be able to say "p"?

Around 3 years old. That figure is the 90% mastery age from McLeod & Crowe (2018), meaning 90% of typically developing children produce "p" correctly in every position in a word by then. The 10% who take longer are still typical.

My child is younger than that and can't say "p". Should I worry?

No. A child below the expected age who isn't producing a sound is doing exactly what the research predicts. It is worth mentioning to your doctor if the sound is still absent well after 3 years old, or if you or other people often can't understand what your child is saying — that second one matters more than any single sound.

How do I practise the "p" sound at home?

Say the word yourself, clearly and unhurried, and let your child have a go. Don't correct a wrong attempt — say the word again warmly and move on. Practice works because it is repeated and pleasant, not because it is accurate. Kide is built on exactly that rule: nothing is scored, and there is no way to fail.

Which words are best for practising "p"?

Short, concrete words for things a child can picture: path, plank, pole, apple, pillow. Every word on this page was checked to be sure the "p" sound really does occur where we say it does.
