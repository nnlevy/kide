Speech sounds

The “p” sound

Most children can say “p” reliably by about 3 years old. Crowe & McLeod (2020) place the 90% mean for this sound in the 2;0–2;11 band among children acquiring English in the United States (https://doi.org/10.1044/2020_AJSLP-19-00168). If yours can't yet and they're younger than that, they are on time.

The short answer

“p” is one of the first sounds children get, usually by around 3 years old. If what you are hearing is “big” for “pig”, the lips are already doing the right thing — only the voice is in the wrong place. Crowe & McLeod (2020) (https://doi.org/10.1044/2020_AJSLP-19-00168).

How “p” is made

Both lips press shut, pressure builds behind them, and they pop apart with a little burst of air.

This one is easy to show. Sit somewhere you can both see a mirror, or just get face to face, and let your child watch you make it — everything that matters happens on the front of your face.

“p” and “b” are the same movement. For both, the lips do exactly the same thing — the voice is switched off for "p" and on for "b". Put a hand on your throat and say them one after the other: one buzzes and one doesn't. If your child has one of the pair they already own the mouth position for the other, which is a much smaller gap than it sounds.

A puff of air you can actually see

Hold a strip of tissue paper in front of your mouth and say "pig". It flutters. Say "big" and it barely moves. That puff is the entire difference between the two sounds — the lips, the tongue and the jaw are doing something identical. So a child who says "big" for "pig" is not missing a sound at all. They have the whole mouth movement already and are simply leaving the voice running through it, which is why this one tends to sort itself out early and quietly.

Try this. Tear a strip of tissue, hold it at your lips, and take turns making it dance. It turns an invisible difference into a game with a visible answer.

Where this age comes from

McLeod & Crowe (2018) reviewed 60 articles describing 64 studies of 26,007 children in 27 languages (https://doi.org/10.1044/2018_AJSLP-17-0100). For English, using a 90%–100% criterion across eight studies, they report mean ages in year-long bands. When a study's ages differed by word position, they recorded the youngest age. They did not use an all-positions rule for every study.

For “p”, Crowe & McLeod (2020) place the 90% mean in the 2;0–2;11 band (https://doi.org/10.1044/2020_AJSLP-19-00168). “By about 3 years old” is the birthday just after that band, which is the conservative way to say it to a parent. It is not a claim that every study found that exact month.

A 50% or 75% criterion lands earlier. Crowe & McLeod (2020) tabulate those criteria next to the 90% figures (https://doi.org/10.1044/2020_AJSLP-19-00168). We talk about the later criterion so an early chart does not turn ordinary acquisition into a worry.

If your child says “big” instead of “pig”

Swapping “b” in for “p” is a pattern clinicians call voicing. Hearing that swap while the sound is still settling is ordinary.

This page does not give that pattern its own age. The age that is published, and linked above, is for the sound itself. If the swap is still the main way your child says the word well after that, mention it — not because the pattern's name means something is wrong, but because that is when someone should listen properly.

Don't correct it in the moment. Saying “no, say pig” teaches a child that talking is a test they can fail, and a child who thinks that talks less. Say the word yourself, warmly, and carry on — they get the model without the verdict.

How to practice without it becoming a test

Say the word yourself first, clearly and slowly. Let your child try. If what comes back isn't right, don't correct it — say the word again warmly, and carry on. A child who feels tested stops trying, and a child who stops trying gets no practice at all.

Common questions

My child says "big" instead of "pig". Is that a problem with their lips?

No — the lips are doing the right thing. "p" and "b" are made identically; the only difference is whether the voice box is running. Your child has the movement and is leaving the voice on. That is called voicing, and it is one of the earliest patterns to settle on its own.

My child says "big" instead of "pig". Is that normal?

Yes. Hearing "big" for "pig" is a familiar pattern, called voicing. Don't correct it in the moment; say the word yourself and move on.

When should my child be able to say "p"?

Around 3 years old. Crowe & McLeod (2020) (https://doi.org/10.1044/2020_AJSLP-19-00168) place the 90% mean for this sound in the 2;0–2;11 band for children acquiring English in the United States. Children who take longer are still typical.

My child is younger than that and can't say "p". Should I worry?

No. A child younger than the age above who isn't producing the sound is doing what the studies describe. It is worth mentioning to your doctor if the sound is still absent well after 3 years old, or if you or other people often can't understand what your child is saying — that second one matters more than any single sound. Crowe & McLeod (2020) (https://doi.org/10.1044/2020_AJSLP-19-00168).

How do I practice the "p" sound at home?

Say the word yourself, clearly and unhurried, and let your child have a go. Don't correct a wrong attempt — say the word again warmly and move on. Practice works because it is repeated and pleasant, not because it is accurate. Kide is built on exactly that rule: nothing is scored, and there is no way to fail.

Which words are best for practicing "p"?

Short, concrete words for things a child can picture: path, plank, pole, apple, pillow. Every word on this page was checked to be sure the "p" sound really does occur where we say it does.
