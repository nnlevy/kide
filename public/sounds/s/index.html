Speech sounds

The “s” sound

Most children are not expected to have a tidy “s” early. This page says by about 5 and a half so a parent is not told to worry at the first late week. Crowe & McLeod (2020) found a 90% mean of 51.33 months for this sound, with studies ranging from 24 to 84 months (https://doi.org/10.1044/2020_AJSLP-19-00168). Five and a half sits inside that spread, not on the average. If yours can't yet and they're younger than that, they are on time.

The short answer

“s” is one of the later sounds, and “tun” for “sun” at four is on time. The one thing genuinely worth listening for is whether the sound is wet and slushy rather than simply off, because those two go in different directions. Crowe & McLeod (2020) (https://doi.org/10.1044/2020_AJSLP-19-00168).

How “s” is made

The tongue tip sits close to — not touching — the ridge behind the top teeth, and air travels down a narrow groove along the middle of the tongue.

You can show part of this one. Some of what matters happens on the front of the face and some of it happens behind the teeth, so a mirror helps a little — but do not expect watching alone to be enough.

“s” and “z” are the same movement. For both, the tongue makes the same narrow groove — the voice runs for "z" and stops for "s". Put a hand on your throat and say them one after the other: one buzzes and one doesn't. If your child has one of the pair they already own the mouth position for the other, which is a much smaller gap than it sounds.

Two different lisps, and only one sorts itself out

This is the one page on this site where the useful thing is a distinction, so here it is plainly. If your child pushes their tongue between their teeth and "sun" comes out as "thun", that is an interdental lisp: common, and it often eases as other sounds settle. If instead the sound comes out wet or slushy, with the air escaping over the sides of the tongue rather than down the middle, that is a lateral lisp — and a lateral lisp is not a stage children pass through. It does not usually resolve by itself at any age, so it is worth mentioning to a speech-language pathologist whenever you notice it rather than waiting to see. Most parents are never told there are two, and wait out the one that does not go away. Bowen, speech-language-therapy.com (https://www.speech-language-therapy.com/clinical-topics/lisping-when-s-and-z-are-hard-to-say).

Try this. Snake play — a long "ssss" with teeth lightly closed. Closed teeth make the between-the-teeth version physically difficult without anyone being corrected.

Where this age comes from

McLeod & Crowe (2018) reviewed 60 articles describing 64 studies of 26,007 children in 27 languages (https://doi.org/10.1044/2018_AJSLP-17-0100). For English, using a 90%–100% criterion across eight studies, they report mean ages in year-long bands. When a study's ages differed by word position, they recorded the youngest age. They did not use an all-positions rule for every study.

For “s”, Crowe & McLeod (2020) report a 90% mean of 51.33 months, and the 12 studies ranged from 24 to 84 months (https://doi.org/10.1044/2020_AJSLP-19-00168). “By about 5 and a half” sits inside that spread. It is not the average those studies found.

A 50% or 75% criterion lands earlier. Crowe & McLeod (2020) tabulate those criteria next to the 90% figures (https://doi.org/10.1044/2020_AJSLP-19-00168). We talk about the later criterion so an early chart does not turn ordinary acquisition into a worry.

If your child says “tun” instead of “sun”

Swapping “t” in for “s” is a pattern clinicians call stopping. Hearing that swap while the sound is still settling is ordinary.

This page does not give that pattern its own age. The age that is published, and linked above, is for the sound itself. If the swap is still the main way your child says the word well after that, mention it — not because the pattern's name means something is wrong, but because that is when someone should listen properly.

Don't correct it in the moment. Saying “no, say sun” teaches a child that talking is a test they can fail, and a child who thinks that talks less. Say the word yourself, warmly, and carry on — they get the model without the verdict.

How to practice without it becoming a test

Say the word yourself first, clearly and slowly. Let your child try. If what comes back isn't right, don't correct it — say the word again warmly, and carry on. A child who feels tested stops trying, and a child who stops trying gets no practice at all.

Common questions

How do I tell which kind of lisp my child has?

Listen for where the air goes. Tongue poking between the teeth with a "th"-like sound is the interdental kind, and it often eases as other sounds settle. A wet, slushy sound with air spilling over the sides of the tongue is a lateral lisp, and that one does not typically resolve on its own — worth raising whenever you notice it. Bowen, speech-language-therapy.com (https://www.speech-language-therapy.com/clinical-topics/lisping-when-s-and-z-are-hard-to-say).

My five-year-old still says "thun" for "sun". Is that still fine?

It is past the age most children have moved on, so it is reasonable to mention it at your next appointment. That is not cause for alarm — plenty of children get there a little later — it just means a professional is now the right person to ask rather than the internet.

My child says "tun" instead of "sun". Is that normal?

Yes. Hearing "tun" for "sun" is a familiar pattern, called stopping. Don't correct it in the moment; say the word yourself and move on.

When should my child be able to say "s"?

This page says around 5 and a half. Crowe & McLeod (2020) (https://doi.org/10.1044/2020_AJSLP-19-00168) report a 90% mean of 51.33 months for this sound, and the studies ranged from 24 to 84 months. Children who take longer than the mean are still part of typical acquisition.

My child is younger than that and can't say "s". Should I worry?

No. A child younger than the age above who isn't producing the sound is doing what the studies describe. It is worth mentioning to your doctor if the sound is still absent well after 5 and a half, or if you or other people often can't understand what your child is saying — that second one matters more than any single sound. Crowe & McLeod (2020) (https://doi.org/10.1044/2020_AJSLP-19-00168).

How do I practice the "s" sound at home?

Say the word yourself, clearly and unhurried, and let your child have a go. Don't correct a wrong attempt — say the word again warmly and move on. Practice works because it is repeated and pleasant, not because it is accurate. Kide is built on exactly that rule: nothing is scored, and there is no way to fail.

Which words are best for practicing "s"?

Short, concrete words for things a child can picture: stone, stick, sun, star, sled. Every word on this page was checked to be sure the "s" sound really does occur where we say it does.
