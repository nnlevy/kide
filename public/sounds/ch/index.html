Speech sounds

The “ch” sound

Children can usually say “ch” reliably by about 5 years old. Crowe & McLeod (2020) place the 90% mean for this sound in the 4;0–4;11 band among children acquiring English in the United States (https://doi.org/10.1044/2020_AJSLP-19-00168). If yours can't yet and they're younger than that, they are on time.

The short answer

“ch” is one of the later sounds. “tip” for “chip” at four is not a missing sound — it is the first half of one, produced correctly and then stopped. Crowe & McLeod (2020) (https://doi.org/10.1044/2020_AJSLP-19-00168).

How “ch” is made

A "t" that does not fully release — instead it opens straight into "sh", in one movement.

You can show part of this one. Some of what matters happens on the front of the face and some of it happens behind the teeth, so a mirror helps a little — but do not expect watching alone to be enough.

"ch" is two sounds welded together

This explains the substitution better than anything else on the page. "ch" is not a single gesture, it is two joined at speed: the tongue makes a "t", and instead of popping cleanly it slides into "sh". A child who says "tip" for "chip" has produced the first half correctly and stopped. They are not missing the sound; they are stopping halfway through it. Which is also why it arrives late — it needs two things the child already owns, joined with timing they do not own yet.

Try this. Say "t — sh" slowly with a gap, then close the gap a bit each time until it becomes "ch". A train pulling away makes the same accelerating pattern and is more fun.

Where this age comes from

McLeod & Crowe (2018) reviewed 60 articles describing 64 studies of 26,007 children in 27 languages (https://doi.org/10.1044/2018_AJSLP-17-0100). For English, using a 90%–100% criterion across eight studies, they report mean ages in year-long bands. When a study's ages differed by word position, they recorded the youngest age. They did not use an all-positions rule for every study.

For “ch”, Crowe & McLeod (2020) place the 90% mean in the 4;0–4;11 band (https://doi.org/10.1044/2020_AJSLP-19-00168). “By about 5 years old” is the birthday just after that band, which is the conservative way to say it to a parent. It is not a claim that every study found that exact month.

A 50% or 75% criterion lands earlier. Crowe & McLeod (2020) tabulate those criteria next to the 90% figures (https://doi.org/10.1044/2020_AJSLP-19-00168). We talk about the later criterion so an early chart does not turn ordinary acquisition into a worry.

If your child says “tip” instead of “chip”

Swapping “t” in for “ch” is a pattern clinicians call stopping. Hearing that swap while the sound is still settling is ordinary.

This page does not give that pattern its own age. The age that is published, and linked above, is for the sound itself. If the swap is still the main way your child says the word well after that, mention it — not because the pattern's name means something is wrong, but because that is when someone should listen properly.

Don't correct it in the moment. Saying “no, say chip” teaches a child that talking is a test they can fail, and a child who thinks that talks less. Say the word yourself, warmly, and carry on — they get the model without the verdict.

How to practice without it becoming a test

Say the word yourself first, clearly and slowly. Let your child try. If what comes back isn't right, don't correct it — say the word again warmly, and carry on. A child who feels tested stops trying, and a child who stops trying gets no practice at all.

Common questions

My child says "tip" for "chip". Are they missing the sound completely?

No — they are producing the first half of it. "ch" is a "t" that slides into "sh"; your child is making the "t" and stopping there. Saying "t—sh" slowly and gradually speeding it up joins the two halves they already have.

My child says "tip" instead of "chip". Is that normal?

Yes. Hearing "tip" for "chip" is a familiar pattern, called stopping. Don't correct it in the moment; say the word yourself and move on.

When should my child be able to say "ch"?

Around 5 years old. Crowe & McLeod (2020) (https://doi.org/10.1044/2020_AJSLP-19-00168) place the 90% mean for this sound in the 4;0–4;11 band for children acquiring English in the United States. Children who take longer are still typical.

My child is younger than that and can't say "ch". Should I worry?

No. A child younger than the age above who isn't producing the sound is doing what the studies describe. It is worth mentioning to your doctor if the sound is still absent well after 5 years old, or if you or other people often can't understand what your child is saying — that second one matters more than any single sound. Crowe & McLeod (2020) (https://doi.org/10.1044/2020_AJSLP-19-00168).

How do I practice the "ch" sound at home?

Say the word yourself, clearly and unhurried, and let your child have a go. Don't correct a wrong attempt — say the word again warmly and move on. Practice works because it is repeated and pleasant, not because it is accurate. Kide is built on exactly that rule: nothing is scored, and there is no way to fail.
