Speech sounds

The “g” sound

Most children can say “g” reliably by about 3 and a half. Crowe & McLeod (2020) place the 90% mean for this sound in the 3;0–3;11 band among children acquiring English in the United States (https://doi.org/10.1044/2020_AJSLP-19-00168). If yours can't yet and they're younger than that, they are on time.

The short answer

“g” usually arrives by around 3 and a half. “doe” for “go” before then is expected: the back of the tongue is the last part of the mouth to come under a child's control. Crowe & McLeod (2020) (https://doi.org/10.1044/2020_AJSLP-19-00168).

How “g” is made

The back of the tongue rises to the soft palate with the voice already running, then lets go.

You cannot show your child this one. All of it happens out of sight inside the mouth, so "watch my mouth" is advice that cannot work here — which is worth knowing before you spend an evening trying.

“g” and “k” are the same movement. For both, the back of the tongue meets the same spot — the voice runs for "g" and stops for "k". Put a hand on your throat and say them one after the other: one buzzes and one doesn't. If your child has one of the pair they already own the mouth position for the other, which is a much smaller gap than it sounds.

The sound that hides at the back of the mouth

Like its twin, "g" happens entirely out of view, so a child has no way to copy it by watching. What they do instead is substitute the closest sound they can place with confidence — the tongue tip — and "go" becomes "doe". Notice that the swap is not random or lazy: it keeps the voicing, keeps the rhythm, and moves only the one thing the child cannot yet aim at. That is a child solving a problem, not failing a task.

Try this. Gargle-play at bath time, or a dramatic "glug glug glug" as a cup empties. Both put the tongue in the right place while nobody is being asked to say anything.

Where this age comes from

McLeod & Crowe (2018) reviewed 60 articles describing 64 studies of 26,007 children in 27 languages (https://doi.org/10.1044/2018_AJSLP-17-0100). For English, using a 90%–100% criterion across eight studies, they report mean ages in year-long bands. When a study's ages differed by word position, they recorded the youngest age. They did not use an all-positions rule for every study.

For “g”, Crowe & McLeod (2020) place the 90% mean in the 3;0–3;11 band (https://doi.org/10.1044/2020_AJSLP-19-00168). “By about 3 and a half” is a point inside that band. It is not a claim that every study found that exact month.

A 50% or 75% criterion lands earlier. Crowe & McLeod (2020) tabulate those criteria next to the 90% figures (https://doi.org/10.1044/2020_AJSLP-19-00168). We talk about the later criterion so an early chart does not turn ordinary acquisition into a worry.

If your child says “doe” instead of “go”

Swapping “d” in for “g” is a pattern clinicians call velar fronting. Hearing that swap while the sound is still settling is ordinary.

This page does not give that pattern its own age. The age that is published, and linked above, is for the sound itself. If the swap is still the main way your child says the word well after that, mention it — not because the pattern's name means something is wrong, but because that is when someone should listen properly.

Don't correct it in the moment. Saying “no, say go” teaches a child that talking is a test they can fail, and a child who thinks that talks less. Say the word yourself, warmly, and carry on — they get the model without the verdict.

How to practice without it becoming a test

Say the word yourself first, clearly and slowly. Let your child try. If what comes back isn't right, don't correct it — say the word again warmly, and carry on. A child who feels tested stops trying, and a child who stops trying gets no practice at all.

Common questions

My child says "doe" for "go" and "dot" for "got". Same issue?

Yes, the same one. The back of the tongue is not in play yet, so the tongue tip stands in for it. The child keeps everything else about the word intact and moves only the part they cannot yet aim at.

My child says "doe" instead of "go". Is that normal?

Yes. Hearing "doe" for "go" is a familiar pattern, called velar fronting. Don't correct it in the moment; say the word yourself and move on.

When should my child be able to say "g"?

Around 3 and a half. Crowe & McLeod (2020) (https://doi.org/10.1044/2020_AJSLP-19-00168) place the 90% mean for this sound in the 3;0–3;11 band for children acquiring English in the United States. Children who take longer are still typical.

My child is younger than that and can't say "g". Should I worry?

No. A child younger than the age above who isn't producing the sound is doing what the studies describe. It is worth mentioning to your doctor if the sound is still absent well after 3 and a half, or if you or other people often can't understand what your child is saying — that second one matters more than any single sound. Crowe & McLeod (2020) (https://doi.org/10.1044/2020_AJSLP-19-00168).

How do I practice the "g" sound at home?

Say the word yourself, clearly and unhurried, and let your child have a go. Don't correct a wrong attempt — say the word again warmly and move on. Practice works because it is repeated and pleasant, not because it is accurate. Kide is built on exactly that rule: nothing is scored, and there is no way to fail.

Which words are best for practicing "g"?

Short, concrete words for things a child can picture: glow, goose, gate. Every word on this page was checked to be sure the "g" sound really does occur where we say it does.
