Speech sounds

The “k” sound

Children can usually say “k” reliably by about 3 and a half. Crowe & McLeod (2020) place the 90% mean for this sound in the 3;0–3;11 band among children acquiring English in the United States (https://doi.org/10.1044/2020_AJSLP-19-00168). If yours can't yet and they're younger than that, they are on time.

The short answer

“k” usually arrives by around 3 and a half. “tat” for “cat” before then is a familiar, documented swap in child speech — and there is a reason a mirror never helps with it. Crowe & McLeod (2020) (https://doi.org/10.1044/2020_AJSLP-19-00168).

How “k” is made

The BACK of the tongue humps up to meet the soft palate, right at the back of the mouth, and releases.

You cannot show your child this one. All of it happens out of sight inside the mouth, so "watch my mouth" is advice that cannot work here — which is worth knowing before you spend an evening trying.

“k” and “g” are the same movement. For both, the back of the tongue meets the same spot — the voice stops for "k" and runs for "g". Put a hand on your throat and say them one after the other: one buzzes and one doesn't. If your child has one of the pair they already own the mouth position for the other, which is a much smaller gap than it sounds.

Why you cannot show your child this one in a mirror

Every piece of advice that starts "sit in front of a mirror and watch my mouth" is useless here, and being told that saves a lot of frustrating evenings. There is nothing to see: the whole event happens behind the tongue, out of sight, with the lips and jaw doing nothing informative. That invisibility is the reason children substitute the nearest sound they CAN see and feel — "tat" for "cat" — and the reason this particular swap is studied throughout child speech.

Try this. Lie your child on their back on the floor and play there for a bit. Gravity pulls the tongue body backwards, and the sound often turns up on its own without anyone having to explain where it lives. A pretend cough — "ackk" — works the same way.

Where this age comes from

McLeod & Crowe (2018) reviewed 60 articles describing 64 studies of 26,007 children in 27 languages (https://doi.org/10.1044/2018_AJSLP-17-0100). For English, using a 90%–100% criterion across eight studies, they report mean ages in year-long bands. When a study's ages differed by word position, they recorded the youngest age. They did not use an all-positions rule for every study.

For “k”, Crowe & McLeod (2020) place the 90% mean in the 3;0–3;11 band (https://doi.org/10.1044/2020_AJSLP-19-00168). “By about 3 and a half” is a point inside that band. It is not a claim that every study found that exact month.

A 50% or 75% criterion lands earlier. Crowe & McLeod (2020) tabulate those criteria next to the 90% figures (https://doi.org/10.1044/2020_AJSLP-19-00168). We talk about the later criterion so an early chart does not turn ordinary acquisition into a worry.

If your child says “tat” instead of “cat”

Swapping “t” in for “k” is a pattern clinicians call velar fronting. Hearing that swap while the sound is still settling is ordinary.

This page does not give that pattern its own age. The age that is published, and linked above, is for the sound itself. If the swap is still the main way your child says the word well after that, mention it — not because the pattern's name means something is wrong, but because that is when someone should listen properly.

Don't correct it in the moment. Saying “no, say cat” teaches a child that talking is a test they can fail, and a child who thinks that talks less. Say the word yourself, warmly, and carry on — they get the model without the verdict.

How to practice without it becoming a test

Say the word yourself first, clearly and slowly. Let your child try. If what comes back isn't right, don't correct it — say the word again warmly, and carry on. A child who feels tested stops trying, and a child who stops trying gets no practice at all.

Common questions

My child says "tat" for "cat". Everyone tells me to use a mirror.

A mirror will not help with this one — "k" is made at the back of the tongue where there is nothing to see. That is exactly why children swap in a sound they can see and feel. Lying on their back, or a pretend cough, gets at it far better than watching a mouth.

At what point is "tat" for "cat" worth mentioning?

Sounds made at the back of the tongue are typically in place somewhere between two and four. If you are hearing no back-of-tongue sounds at all once your child is three, that is worth raising with your doctor — not as an emergency, but because it is one of the few early signs that is genuinely useful to check.

My child says "tat" instead of "cat". Is that normal?

Yes. Hearing "tat" for "cat" is a familiar pattern, called velar fronting. Don't correct it in the moment; say the word yourself and move on.

When should my child be able to say "k"?

Around 3 and a half. Crowe & McLeod (2020) (https://doi.org/10.1044/2020_AJSLP-19-00168) place the 90% mean for this sound in the 3;0–3;11 band for children acquiring English in the United States. Children who take longer are still typical.

My child is younger than that and can't say "k". Should I worry?

No. A child younger than the age above who isn't producing the sound is doing what the studies describe. It is worth mentioning to your doctor if the sound is still absent well after 3 and a half, or if you or other people often can't understand what your child is saying — that second one matters more than any single sound. Crowe & McLeod (2020) (https://doi.org/10.1044/2020_AJSLP-19-00168).

How do I practice the "k" sound at home?

Say the word yourself, clearly and unhurried, and let your child have a go. Don't correct a wrong attempt — say the word again warmly and move on. Practice works because it is repeated and pleasant, not because it is accurate. Kide is built on exactly that rule: nothing is scored, and there is no way to fail.

Which words to practice for "k"?

Short, concrete words for things a child can picture: kite, candle, cat, key, golden key. Every word on this page was checked to be sure the "k" sound really does occur where we say it does.
