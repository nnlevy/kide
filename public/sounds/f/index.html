Speech sounds

The “f” sound

Most children can say “f” reliably by about 4 years old. If yours can't yet and they're younger than that, they are on time.

The short answer

“f” usually settles by around 4 years old. “pish” for “fish” is a normal way-station — the right lips, held for the wrong length of time.

How “f” is made

Top teeth rest lightly on the bottom lip and air hisses out between them.

This one is easy to show. Sit somewhere you can both see a mirror, or just get face to face, and let your child watch you make it — everything that matters happens on the front of your face.

“f” and “v” are the same movement. For both, the teeth and lip do the same thing — the voice runs for "v" and stops for "f". Put a hand on your throat and say them one after the other: one buzzes and one doesn't. If your child has one of the pair they already own the mouth position for the other, which is a much smaller gap than it sounds.

Sounds you can stretch, and sounds that pop

Up to this point almost every sound a child owns is a pop: lips or tongue close, pressure builds, it releases. "f" is a different kind of thing entirely — you can hold it for as long as you have breath. That distinction, between sounds that burst and sounds you can stretch, is the actual developmental step here, and it is why "pish" for "fish" is such a common way-station: the child has the right lip and teeth but is still releasing it as a pop instead of letting it run.

Try this. Have a competition to hold "ffffff" the longest. It is the one sound at this stage you can compete on for duration, and length is the exact thing being learned.

Where the “f” figure of 4 years old comes from

McLeod & Crowe (2018) reviewed consonant acquisition across 64 studies and reported the age by which 90% of typically developing children produce each sound correctly — in the beginning, middle and end of words. For “f” that age is 4;00 (4 years old).

The 90% figure is deliberately conservative. Older charts used 50% or 75%, which produce much earlier ages and flag ordinary late acquisition as a problem. We use the conservative number on purpose: an earlier one would manufacture worry rather than answer it.

If your child says “pish” instead of “fish”

Swapping “p” in for “f” is not a random mistake. It has a name — stopping — and it is one of the most common and best documented patterns in young children's speech. Nearly every child does some version of it.

It usually sorts itself out by around 3 and a half. Before then it is a normal stage, not an error to be corrected. If it is still there well after that, it is worth mentioning — not because something is wrong, but because that is when someone should take a proper look.

Don't correct it in the moment. Saying “no, say fish” teaches a child that talking is a test they can fail, and a child who thinks that talks less. Say the word yourself, warmly, and carry on — they get the model without the verdict.

How to practise without it becoming a test

Say the word yourself first, clearly and slowly. Let your child try. If what comes back isn't right, don't correct it — say the word again warmly, and carry on. A child who feels tested stops trying, and a child who stops trying gets no practice at all.

Common questions

My child says "pish" for "fish". What are they actually doing?

Turning a stretchy sound into a pop. The lips and teeth are close to right; the difference is that "f" needs to run continuously and "p" bursts. Holding a long "ffff" playfully targets exactly the part that is missing.

My child says "pish" instead of "fish". Is that normal?

Yes — very. Substituting "p" for "f" is a documented pattern called stopping, and it typically resolves on its own by around 3 and a half. Don't correct it in the moment; just say the word yourself and move on.

When should my child be able to say "f"?

Around 4 years old. That figure is the 90% mastery age from McLeod & Crowe (2018), meaning 90% of typically developing children produce "f" correctly in every position in a word by then. The 10% who take longer are still typical.

My child is younger than that and can't say "f". Should I worry?

No. A child below the expected age who isn't producing a sound is doing exactly what the research predicts. It is worth mentioning to your doctor if the sound is still absent well after 4 years old, or if you or other people often can't understand what your child is saying — that second one matters more than any single sound.

How do I practise the "f" sound at home?

Say the word yourself, clearly and unhurried, and let your child have a go. Don't correct a wrong attempt — say the word again warmly and move on. Practice works because it is repeated and pleasant, not because it is accurate. Kide is built on exactly that rule: nothing is scored, and there is no way to fail.
