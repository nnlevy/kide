Privacy

Why we don't send your child's voice anywhere

The easy way to build "say the answer out loud" is to record your child's voice and send it to a speech-recognition company's servers. We ruled that out on day one, which meant ruling out the easy way to build this.

The rule we actually build to is one line: if a browser can't understand speech without sending it to a server, we don't turn the microphone on there at all. Not "we ask before sending" — we simply don't offer listening as an option on that device. There is no setting that enables a cloud fallback, because there is no cloud fallback in the code to enable.

What that looks like today

Before Pip ever listens, three things all have to be true: the browser reports an on-device speech model is actually installed and ready; a grown-up has been through the Parent Corner and read the consent screen; and — checked again, separately, because the first check isn't always trustworthy — the on-device-only flag we set is confirmed to have taken effect, not silently ignored.

In Pip's garden — the colours, counting and shapes games — that combination exists on Chrome 139 and newer, on desktop and Android. On an iPad the microphone doesn't appear there at all.

In Say it with me it now works on iPad too, by a different route: instead of asking the browser to recognise speech, we ship our own small speech model and run it inside the page. It never contacts a server because there is nothing to contact — the model is on the device, in the tab, and the audio never leaves the function that scores it. We measured it on a real iPad before relying on it: 117 milliseconds to score a word, which is faster than a child notices.

Tapping to answer works exactly the same on every device, so nothing is ever degraded by a "no" — it is simply the version of the game that device gets.

What "nothing leaves the device" actually means

When listening is on, there's no recording, no upload, and nothing saved. What your child says becomes a word for a few milliseconds — just long enough to compare it against the two to four answers already on the screen — and then it's gone. We'd rather a mumbled answer get a gentle "I didn't quite hear that, try again?" than guess wrong, so ties and low-confidence guesses are always treated as "didn't hear it," never as a coin flip.

Speaking, not just listening

The microphone is only half of it. A child who cannot read needs the game to talk, and in Say it with me every line is now spoken as well as shown. That speech is subject to the same one-line rule: it is produced on the device, and if your browser only offers an online voice — one that would send the words to a server to be read — we decline it and show the line as text instead.

Being precise about what you'll hear today: the lines are pre-recorded and delivered as ordinary audio files, so what your child hears is the same on every device and is not generated while they play. If a clip can't be fetched, the browser's own built-in voice reads the line instead — and if that isn't available either, the line is shown as text. The Parent Corner tells you which of the three your device is actually using rather than making you guess.

What we're testing for the devices this doesn't cover yet

The iPad gap is now closed for the word practice, and the honest caveat has moved rather than disappeared. Running our own model tells us how well the sounds in a word match what was expected. That is genuinely useful over weeks, and it is not a judgement about any single attempt — automated scoring of young children's speech misses real mistakes far more often than it invents them, which is exactly why your child is never told they got a word wrong, and why the third try always works out regardless.

Two things are still true and worth saying plainly. The thresholds behind that scoring were tuned on adult recordings, not children's, so they are a sensible starting point rather than a finished calibration. And Pip's original garden games still use the browser's own recogniser, so on an iPad they remain tap-only. Both will change; neither will change the one-line rule: it's your child's voice, so it stays on your child's device, or the feature doesn't ship at all.
