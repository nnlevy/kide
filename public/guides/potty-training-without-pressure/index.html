Habits & routines

Why Pip's potty routine is never scored

A toddler plays happily with a play sink that has no water. Nothing is rewarded, nothing is scored — and the play continues anyway. That single observation is the whole design behind Pip's Turn.

What a child is actually enjoying with that empty sink is being the person who does the routine. Pretend play is how toddlers rehearse sequences they've watched adults perform. The motivation is agency and imitation — not an outcome. So in Pip's Turn, your child helps Pip go: walks Pip to the bathroom, sits Pip down, waits, wipes, flushes, washes hands. A short sequence of taps, no wrong answers, no score.

Why not just add a reward for going?

Because the research on rewarding prosocial behavior runs the other way. In Warneken and Tomasello (2008), 36 German children around 20 months old who had been given a material reward for helping helped less afterward — 53% of trials once the reward was withdrawn, compared with 81% after praise and 89% when nothing was given (Developmental Psychology, https://doi.org/10.1037/a0013860).

What this means for Pip: the leaf-and-streak system that works fine for a colors-and-shapes quiz is never extended to potty training or handwashing. A right answer is a right answer — but pooping isn't a right answer, and treating it like one is where the harm starts.

Why pressure is the actual risk, not just an annoyance

In a Flanders survey of 1,218 questionnaires, Naenen and colleagues (2025) found that 23.6% of children refused to defecate on a potty during toilet training, and that this stool-toileting refusal was associated with 3.2 times the odds of functional constipation (95% confidence interval 2.3 to 4.3). That is an association, not a demonstration that pressure causes the constipation. Pip's Turn does not turn elimination into a scored event (https://doi.org/10.1097/dbp.0000000000001370).

So in Pip's Turn: Pip sometimes sits and nothing happens. Pip sometimes doesn't make it in time. Both are ordinary endings, not failures — nobody in the game is disappointed either way. If that happens to your child in real life, they've already watched it be okay for someone else.

Why there's no screen in the bathroom

Pip's Turn is rehearsal, played before the real moment, in another room — never handed to a child sitting on the actual toilet. A tablet there pulls attention away from the internal signals a child is supposed to be learning to notice, and can stretch sitting time well past the point of usefulness. We say this to parents directly rather than leaving it implied: this app's job ends where the bathroom begins.

When it's not about motivation anymore

Withholding that goes on for more than a few days, pain, blood, or a child who was previously trained starting to have accidents again — these are medical questions, not motivational ones, and worth a pediatrician's attention rather than more rehearsal. We'd rather say that plainly than pretend an app can tell the difference.
